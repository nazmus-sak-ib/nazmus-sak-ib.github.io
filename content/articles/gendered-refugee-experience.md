## Abstract

Refugees live through a myriad of premigration and post-migration experiences that do not conform to fundamental human rights. Their access to safety is especially denied during their extended camp life. The violations are often so severe that they transgress the borders of age, gender, and social status. When the host country keeps the refugees for an extended period, they are often seen to get engaged in illicit activities to escape the restricted status quo or "to survive." Even in this case, researches show great gender iniquity when looked through the gender lens. While male youths are more often seen participating in activities like drug trafficking and terrorism in and outside the camps, their female counterparts are historically seen to get entrapped in sex trafficking and prostitution. Survival sex, which refers to the seemingly voluntary selling of sex in exchange for needs or services a person is otherwise supposed to be entitled to, is another vulnerability that endangers refugee girls, especially the young ones. Historically, refugee women have been seen to take part in survival sex both within and inside the camp in exchange for food, money, or security. The Rohingya have been stuck in Bangladesh for over a decade, and their urge to escape the camp life has engendered newer forms of survival sex. This paper explores such trends as the promise of legalization through marriage, freedom from captivity, or simply getting a job.

## Keywords

Rohingya; Refugee; Gendered Experience; Survival Sex; Violence Against Women

## Introduction

## Refugee Crisis

Refugees are groups of people who have been forced to leave their natural place of habitat due to armed conflicts, civil wars, or natural disasters. Of course, the most significant crises are born from armed clashes between parties when many flee their country to survive. The plight of the refugee extends beyond this inhumane process of leaving to the newly-occupied camps in a host country. Their stay in those countries is often so delayed that it becomes complicated to maintain law and order in the refugee camps. As a result, the refugees struggle to meet their basic human needs. Helps through the support of government and international organizations can be lacking to meet their health, sanitation, and education needs. To add more to their woe, the refugees are often at the receiving end of gross human rights violations. Murders, rapes, and human trafficking have been at the center of the news in the past. However, the research addressing the plight of refugees tended to be gender-blind until some time ago (Pittaway, 2013). Now that more studies are looking at the refugee issue through a gender lens, a staggering inequity between the experiences of male and female refugees is found.

## Refugees Through Gender Lens

Although women are particularly targeted based on their gender (rape in camps, sex trafficking, prostitution) or the roles ascribed to their gender (fetching water or firewood at a long distance from the camp). Evidence of such atrocities is often sketchy and testimonial-based, as the institutionalized nature of a camp makes it "normal." Moreover, it becomes difficult to report such events for fear of ostracization by a woman's own community. The matter worsens when the perpetrators are the border guards or camp personnel. It came as a shock and inspired a zero-tolerance policy response when UN

Peacekeepers were found to sexually exploit and abuse refugee women (for example, Cambodia, Ivory Coast, Congo, Somalia, Haiti, Liberia, and South Sudan) (Mudgway, 2017).

## Survival Sex

Survival sex is undertaken to meet an individual's immediate basic needs, like food, shelter, or medication. It is different from prostitution in a way that the victim is often out of choice, and the provider demands sexual intercourse in exchange for such services that he could demand being in a position of control. The victims mostly constitute the homeless, street children, runaways, and refugees. In fact, survival sex is widespread among refugees. For example, in the case of Uganda, where 1.4 million people have fled their habitats to avoid the conflict between the government and the militant forces, it was reported that the government soldiers and the local defense personnel were forcing the displaced women and girls into survival sex (Human Rights Watch, 2005).

## Rohingya and GBV

Although the Rohingya crisis started in 1982 when the Myanmar government took away their citizenship rights, the latest clash in 2016 caused the biggest influx of approximately one million Rohingya refugees fleeing to Bangladesh. According to UNICEF, nearly two-thirds of the population comprises women and children. Around 16% of the Rohingya households across 34 camps in Cox's Bazar are headed by a female member (Gaynor, 2019). This large number of women face challenges every day from within and outside of their households and camp. Their challenges range from insecurity and violence to restricted movement and freedom of speech within their community (Oxfam). Most of the violence they face is either domestic violence or sexual violence in the forms of harassment and rape, severely worsened by the lack of structure within the camp system (Nordby, 2018).

## Rohingya and Survival Sex

ISCG brief (2018a) mentions that the Rohingya women are in danger of exposing themselves to harmful coping mechanisms, including survival sex. However, studies have explored how the Rohingya crisis related to sex trafficking and the prostitution industry, but the domain of survival sex has not been exclusively studied in this context.

## Literature Review

Women and children constitute 80% of the total refugee population. Yet, gender-sensitive analysis was missing in refugee case studies for a long time (Goodkind & Deacon, 2004; Schlenzka, Sommo, Wadia, & Campani, 2004; Sideris, 2003). A paper titled "Racism, Refugees, and Multi-Ethnic States" was presented at the World Conference against Racism (Geneva, May 2020). The article failed to mention "gender" in its twenty-seven-page-long discourse. The gendered nature of the refugee experience was not added to the UNHCR agenda until 1985. Later approaches of focusing on one common refugee experience overlooked the plight of the women too (e.g., Barakat, 1973; Mezey, 1960).

The recent approaches of incorporating women's perspectives in refugee studies have marked a shift in the pendulum (Annandale & Hunt, 2000). Studies have focused exclusively on the gendered refugee experience of women and have brought forth some new outlooks on the matter (Goodkind & Deacon, 2004).

Case studies in specific incidents have highlighted the gendered experience of women living as refugees in a camp. For example, studies in the Dadaab refugee camp in Kenya revealed that a large of rapes took

place when the women would have to walk a long distance to collect firewood, a gender role they had to carry out (Agier, 2002).

Regarding "survival sex" carried out by men in control of services or goods, refugee women across camps and time are seen to suffer the same fate. O' Brien (2011) found that women in Liberia, Haiti, Ivory Coast and Sierra Leone had to resort to survival sex when they were hungry or under duress in their camps. Oxfam employees were found to be involved in sexual exploitations in post-earthquake Haiti (Omvärlden, 2018). Mudgway summarizes in his study how the UN peacekeepers were alleged to be involved in "survival sex" cases and how it exerted a zero-tolerance policy change as a response.

However, the incidents of survival sex are not limited to the relief or security personnel only. Even the locals in the host region are sometimes found to exchange goods or services with the same intention. Ferris (2007) found that medical workers were taking unfair advantage of their position in exchange for sexual favors in Haiti. Teachers in the camp were also part of this activity.

Researchers have studied the Rohingya crisis from different angles. Nordby (2018) shows the various forms of sexual violence Rohingya women face in their camp life. This violence comes from domestic abuse, sexual harassment, and rape. The occurrence of other forms of violations like sex trafficking, forced prostitution, and teenage pregnancy is also very high in the camps (Hossain, 2018). However, the issue of survival sex in the Rohingya camps has been briefly discussed in studies (Nordby, 2018) but not studied exclusively. One contributing factor to this matter is the sensitivity of this topic and the potential difficulty of carrying out research in the camp.

This study explores how the Rohingya girls are lured into sexual exploitation with the promise of legalization or other services by the locals around the camp. While doing so, I will also focus on how the process of "escaping" camp life differs across gender.

## Methodology

## Data Collection

Due to time and resource limitations, I have employed an online scraping technique, where I will systematically search news and article portals for relevant cases. This study is based on anecdotal accounts published in online reports.

## Limitation

One fundamental limitation of the "raped after enticement of marriage" group of news reports is that it obscures the nuances of the matter, and it cannot be conclusively said to conform to the "survival sex" concept. Only candid personal accounts of the victims can delineate the grey issue.

Secondly, the more pervasive "sex trafficking" and the less focused "survival sex" narratives often overlap in news reports. It becomes challenging to differentiate between them.

Lastly, it goes underreported when the gatekeepers and relief personnel are involved in sexual exploitation due to the matter's sensitivity. This domain is thus left out of the scope of this study.

## Case Study

Faysal is a 15-year-old Rohingya boy living in the Musuni camp in Cox's Bazar. He has managed to sneak out of the camp during the day to work at a hotel in the locality. Faysal's employer is keen to keep him, as he can pay half as he would have to pay any Bangladeshi employee. Despite not being legal, many Rohingya boys and men have found themselves low-wage work outside the camps. Although Sultan Ahmed, a Teknaf local and a member of the Rohingya Resistance Committee, would not be pleased with this employment, the young Rohingya can argue that many youths of his camp have been involved with gang-based terrorism and drug trafficking. This story was published in the Dhaka Post.[^1]

Putting the legal debates aside, Faysal's "way out" of the horrors of the camp is a better story than most. But such was not the case with Tahera Begum[^2], an eighteen-year-old living not too far away from Faysal. Her escape from the camp would come at the cost of agreeing to marry a man who just "has a job." We do not know the ultimate fate of Tahera. But we do see the future of an unnamed fourteen-year-old Rohingya girl, who was raped and left stranded after a local man promised to marry her.[^3]

A general problem that cuts across time and space is that when the refugees flee to a different country, the female population is seen to be equipped with less work experience than their male counterparts. It owes mainly to the norms of their homeland, where women are discouraged from working outside. The Rohingya women suffer from the same problem, compounded by the camps' institutionalized nature. As a result, they cannot show the luxury of trying to circumvent the camp constraints without endangering themselves. The stories of Faysal and Tahera only go to support this argument. The young girls even go as far as onboarding for "marriage odysseys," where they marry older men outside Bangladesh over the phone and later embark on dangerous sea journeys.

In some cases, it is superficially impossible to conclude what "enticement of marriage"[^4] means in the context of refugees. Even then, we must probe into the "why" s to understand how to prevent such occurrences. Conflating "survival sex" cases with sex trafficking might empower the perpetrators even more and leave the refugee girls stranded in a worse position. Although the exact numbers are unavailable, anecdotal stories and news reports are abundant on this matter (Tayeb, 2019; Jushan, 2022).

It is essential to distinguish between sex trafficking, where the girls are hoaxed or coerced into captivity, and survival sex, where the girls consent to a dangerous path that they would not have taken if they had the options. It would only then be possible to take preventive measures and identify the policy gaps to increase the safety of the Rohingya women.

## Conclusion

Much research has been done on the violence against women in the Rohingya community. But unfortunately, little work exists on the issue presented in this paper. This topic should be handled with the attention and sensitivity it requires to improve the living conditions of Rohingya women.

Although the government and humanitarian organizations are working relentlessly to stop GBV in the Rohingya, their effort can only be bolstered by diving deeper into the complexities of the matter.
